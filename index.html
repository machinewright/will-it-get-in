Will it get in?

Buying a sofa, a wardrobe or a mattress? Measure it, and the tight spot on the way in: a hallway corner or a door. You'll see whether it gets through, exactly how to carry it (flat, tilted or on its end), and the longest one that would.

    How it works

    It treats the furniture as a box with the sizes you give, and your hallway and door as walls, a door frame and a ceiling. Then it searches for a way to move the box through: sliding it, turning it, lifting one end and tilting it, standing it on end, laying it on its back, in steps of 5 cm and 5° first and, if that finds nothing, of 2.5 cm and 2.5°. At every step the box is at least 1.5 cm from every wall, the door frame and the ceiling, and between steps it is checked at points so close together that no part of it moves more than half a centimetre unchecked. So when it shows you a way, that way works for the box with at least a centimetre to spare all round, from the moment it reaches the corner or door.

    It assumes the box can get that far: that it arrives from somewhere with room to turn it (outside, or a room), resting on any of its sides, and that it can be tipped there if the ceiling allows (one end at a time, as people do). It doesn't know about radiators, light fittings, banisters or a door that doesn't open fully: measure the narrowest point, with the door open. Stairs aren't covered yet.

    When it finds no way, that is not a proof: a way could squeeze between its steps, or need less than 1.5 cm to spare. Carrying a box flat round a corner has an exact answer you can work out by hand; on the tests the search agrees with it to within a centimetre, and it never found a way that the exact answer rules out. Some doors are settled without searching, by a rule that is certain: at the moment the box's middle passes through the doorway, the line through its middle across the doorway is inside the opening, and every such line through a box is at least as long as its shortest side. So if the shortest side is wider than the doorway less a centimetre each side, no way of turning that box gets it through with that centimetre to spare.

    For a door it first tries carrying it straight through. Then every point of it crosses the doorway at the same height and the same distance from the door frame, so it gets through exactly when its outline, seen face-on to the wall, fits the opening (with the margin). That test is exact for each position it tries: turned in 15° steps and tipped in quarter-degree steps, as far as the ceiling allows. Off a hallway it also needs to fit the hallway's width while you walk it up to the door. Only if that fails does it search.

    The "what would fit" lines come from the same search: a box that long, or a hallway or door that wide, is one it found a way for, and a centimetre more is one it didn't.

    A real sofa isn't a box. If the box gets through, the sofa does too, since it fits inside the box. If it doesn't, tick "It's a sofa" and give its seat height, the depth of its backrest and its arms: it then uses that shape (a seat block, a backrest along the back and two equal arms, all solid), which can go where its box can't, for example tipped back through a door narrower than the sofa is deep. The rule about the shortest side is for boxes only. It still can't see legs that come off (take them off and subtract them from the heights), soft cushions, rounded arms, or the gaps movers use under a raised seat, so a real sofa may still do a little better. Corner sofas and chaises aren't covered, and the search for a sofa shape can take longer.
